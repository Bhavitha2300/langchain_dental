## 1.1 Assessing Bleeding Risk

Before providing dental treatment for a patient taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs, their bleeding risk should be assessed. This involves consideration of both the likely risk of bleeding associated with the required dental procedure and the patient’s individual level of bleeding risk, which can be affected by the anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs that they are taking, in addition to their other medical conditions and medications. These issues are addressed in Sections 1.1.1 and 1.1.2. Guidance on the management of the patient’s dental treatment based on this risk assessment is presented in later sections.

While the risk of bleeding complications associated with dental treatment for these patients should always be considered, it should be noted that existing evidence and clinical experience suggest that serious adverse bleeding events are rare. The incidence of post-operative bleeding events requiring further haemostatic measures, after dental procedures for patients who had continued their oral anticoagulant therapy perioperatively, has been estimated at approximately 4%. Notably, almost all bleeding events were managed with local haemostatic measures and less than 0.1% of patients continuing their anticoagulant required hospitalisation for treatment.


### 1.1.1 Which Dental Procedures Have the Highest Bleeding Risk?

Dental procedures can be categorised into those that are unlikely, under normal circumstances, to cause bleeding and those that can be expected to cause some level of bleeding.

The management of patients taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs whose dental treatment involves procedures from the first category should be straightforward and these patients can be treated according to standard practice, with care taken to avoid causing bleeding.

More careful consideration should be given to patients who require procedures likely to result in bleeding. Dental procedures that are likely to result in bleeding are further categorised into those with a low risk of post-operative bleeding complications and those that are judged to be more invasive and potentially carry a relatively higher risk of bleeding complications.

By bleeding complications, we mean prolonged or excessive bleeding or bleeding not controlled by initial haemostatic measures.

Note that the use of the term "higher risk" is not intended to suggest that these are high risk dental treatments. Furthermore, a dental procedure categorised as low or higher risk is still likely to be considered a relatively minor bleeding risk in the wider context of medical surgical procedures.

The procedure categories (1.1.1.1, 1.1.1.2, 1.1.1.3) are intended to be a guide only and because of the wide variation in invasiveness of some treatments, such as those involving flap raising procedures, there will be some overlap between the categories. Bleeding risk assessment for a patient’s dental treatment is likely to require further judgement on an individual case basis taking into consideration the extent and invasiveness of the procedure.

Before performing a dental procedure that is likely to cause bleeding on a patient taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs, the dentist or dental care professional should use their clinical judgement to determine whether they are sufficiently confident and skilled in the procedure and management of the associated peri-operative bleeding.


#### 1.1.1.1 Dental Procedures That Are Unlikely to Cause Bleeding

- Local anaesthesia by infiltration, intraligamentary or mental nerve block
- Local anaesthesia by inferior dental block or other regional nerve blocks
- Basic periodontal examination (BPE)
- Supragingival removal of plaque, calculus and stain
- Direct or indirect restorations with supragingival margins
- Endodontics - orthograde
- Impressions and other prosthetics procedures
- Fitting and adjustment of orthodontic appliances

Local anaesthesia should be delivered using an aspirating syringe and should include a vasoconstrictor, unless contraindicated. Note that other methods of local anaesthetic delivery are preferred over regional nerve blocks, whether the patient is taking an anticoagulant or not.

There is no evidence to suggest that an inferior dental block performed on an anticoagulated patient poses a significant risk of bleeding. However, for patients taking warfarin, if there are any indications that the patient has an unstable INR, or other signs of excessive anticoagulation, an INR should be requested before the procedure to ensure it is below 4 before proceeding.

Although a basic periodontal examination can result in some bleeding from gingival margins, this is considered extremely unlikely to lead to complications.


#### 1.1.1.2 Dental Procedures That Are Likely to Cause Bleeding: Low Risk of Post-operative Bleeding Complications

- Simple extractions (1-3 teeth, with restricted wound size)
- Incision and drainage of intra-oral swellings
- Detailed six-point full periodontal examination
- Root surface debridement (RSD)
- Direct or indirect restorations with subgingival margins

Simple extractions refers to those that are expected to be straightforward without surgical complications.


#### 1.1.1.3 Dental Procedures That Are Likely to Cause Bleeding: Higher Risk of Post-operative Bleeding Complications

- Complex extractions
- Adjacent extractions that will cause a large wound
- More than 3 extractions at once
- Flap raising procedures, including:
  - Elective surgical extractions
  - Periodontal surgery
  - Preprosthetic surgery
  - Periradicular surgery
  - Crown lengthening
  - Dental implant surgery
- Gingival recontouring
- Biopsies

Complex extractions refers to those that may be likely to have surgical complications.

For flap raising procedures, gingival recontouring and biopsies, consideration should be given to the extent and invasiveness of the individual procedure. Some may be less invasive and could be treated as low risk.


### 1.1.2 Which Patients Have the Highest Bleeding Risk?

A patient’s individual risk of bleeding complications is dependent on a variety of factors, including the type and combination of anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs they are taking, their underlying health conditions and other medications that they may be taking.

The patient’s medical history and details of the prescribed and non-prescribed medication they are taking should be noted at the start of each course of treatment and checked for any changes at each visit (see section 1.1.3).


#### 1.1.2.1 Bleeding Risks Associated with Different Anticoagulants and Antiplatelet Drugs

There is currently insufficient evidence to directly compare with any certainty the bleeding risks for dental treatment associated with the different anticoagulants and antiplatelet medications.

According to the clinical trials conducted by the drug manufacturers, incidences of major spontaneous and procedural bleeding events for patients with atrial fibrillation taking dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban or edoxaban were similar or lower than for those taking warfarin.

Similarly, estimates from very limited data from dental studies suggest that the risk of post-operative bleeding for dental patients taking DOACs is not significantly different to that for patients on vitamin K antagonists including warfarin.

Patients who are on dual or combination therapies and are taking more than one anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug are likely to have a higher bleeding risk than those on single drug therapies.

Direct comparisons of dual versus single antiplatelet therapy suggest that the risks of both peri-operative and post-operative bleeding with minor oral surgery are higher for patients on dual therapy.


#### 1.1.2.2 Bleeding Risks Associated with Other Medical Conditions

Certain medical conditions are known to be associated with an increased bleeding risk, due to effects on either coagulation or platelet function, and should be taken into consideration when planning dental treatment for any patient.

These include liver, kidney and bone marrow disorders. Although these effects are not dependent on the patient’s anticoagulation medication, it is especially important that the dentist recognises these as additional risk factors that can contribute to post-operative bleeding complications in patients taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs.

It is not possible to give an exhaustive list, but the main medical conditions affecting coagulation or platelet function which could be relevant for patients also being treated with anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs are listed below.


##### Chronic Renal Failure

Increased bleeding risk due to associated platelet dysfunction.


##### Liver Disease

Examples include liver disease caused by:

- alcohol dependence
- chronic viral hepatitis
- autoimmune hepatitis
- primary biliary cholangitis

Increased bleeding risk may be due to:

- reduced production of coagulation factors
- reduction in platelet number and function due to splenomegaly
- direct bone marrow toxicity and reduced platelet numbers associated with alcohol excess


##### Haematological Malignancy or Myelodysplastic Disorder

Increased bleeding risk due to impaired coagulation or platelet function, even in remission.


##### Recent or Current Chemotherapy or Radiotherapy

Increased bleeding risk due to pancytopenia including reduced platelet numbers.

This includes patients who have received chemotherapy less than three months ago, or total body irradiation less than six months ago.


##### Advanced Heart Failure

Increased bleeding risk due to resulting liver failure.


##### Inherited Coagulation Disorders

Includes all types of haemophilia and von Willebrand’s disease.

Increased bleeding risk due to defective or reduced levels of coagulation factors.


##### Acquired or Inherited Platelet Disorders

Includes immune thrombocytopenia (ITP).

Increased bleeding risk due to reduced platelet numbers or abnormal platelet function.


##### Connective Tissue Disorders

Includes Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome (EDS).

Increased bleeding risk due to vascular fragility, particularly in the vascular subtype, and platelet function abnormalities in some patients.

For medically complex patients such as these, the patient’s general medical practitioner or specialist should be consulted, to establish the extent of the disease in order to assess the likely impact on the bleeding risk for the dental procedure.


#### 1.1.2.3 Bleeding Risks Associated with Prescribed or Non-prescribed Medications

In addition to the medical conditions discussed above, a number of different medications can exacerbate a patient’s bleeding risk over and above the effects of the anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs they are taking.

Although not an exhaustive list, groups of drugs to be aware of include those described below.


##### Other Anticoagulants or Antiplatelet Drugs

Patients can be on dual, multiple or combined antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapies.

These patients are likely to have a higher risk of bleeding complications than those on single drug regimes.

Be aware that patients may also be taking non-prescribed aspirin, and this antiplatelet agent can in effect convert a prescribed monotherapy into a dual therapy.


##### Cytotoxic Drugs or Drugs Associated with Bone Marrow Suppression

Examples include:

- leflunomide
- hydroxychloroquine
- sulfasalazine
- penicillamine
- gold
- methotrexate
- azathioprine
- mycophenolate

These can reduce platelet numbers and/or impair liver function affecting production of coagulation factors.

Patients with inflammatory bowel disease or autoimmune/rheumatological conditions are commonly prescribed these drugs.


##### Biologic Immunosuppression Therapies

Examples include:

- infliximab
- adalimumab
- etanercept
- tocilizumab
- certolizumab
- abatacept
- anakinra

These can cause thrombocytopenia and/or impair liver function.


##### Non-steroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs

Examples include:

- aspirin
- ibuprofen
- diclofenac
- naproxen

These impair platelet function to various extents.


##### Drugs Affecting the Nervous System

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) have the potential to impair platelet aggregation and, although unlikely to be clinically significant in isolation, may in combination with other antiplatelet drugs increase the bleeding time.

Carbamazepine can affect both liver function and bone marrow production of platelets. Patients most at risk are those recently started on this medication or following dose adjustment.


##### Herbal and Complementary Medicines

Some herbal and complementary medicines may affect bleeding risk, either on their own or when in combination with other anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs.

These include:

- St. John’s Wort
- Ginkgo biloba
- garlic

For the management of patients taking these additional medications, the patient’s prescribing clinician, specialist or general medical practitioner could be consulted in order to assess the likely impact on bleeding risk.


### 1.1.3 Advice for Assessing Bleeding Risk

The following best practice advice is based on clinical experience and expert opinion.

Assess whether the required dental treatment is likely to cause bleeding and, if so, whether it has a low or higher risk of bleeding complications.

Ask the patient about their current or planned use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs and other prescribed and non-prescribed medications, when taking or confirming their medical history.

- The patient should have been advised by their prescriber or dispenser about their anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug(s) and the need to inform their dentist. However, some patients may not know that their medication is an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug.
- Other medications can also affect a patient’s bleeding risk.
- Be aware that many patients take non-prescribed medications such as aspirin, or other NSAIDs. Patients taking these may have a higher bleeding risk.
- Confirming the details of the patient’s medical history in advance, for example by phone or other remote methods, to check for any changes that could impact treatment and require postponement, could reduce wasted appointments and travel.

Ask the patient whether their anticoagulant or antiplatelet treatment is lifelong or for a limited time.

- If the patient is on time-limited medication, it may be possible to delay dental treatment until they have stopped taking the drug or drugs.

Ask the patient about any medical conditions that they have.

- The medical conditions for which anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs are commonly prescribed may indicate that a patient is taking one of these medications.
- Some patients may have other conditions such as kidney or liver disease or bone marrow disorders that can affect their coagulation and platelet function.

Ask the patient about their bleeding history, for example:

- incidences of bleeding requiring retreatment or a hospital visit
- prolonged bleeding from other wounds
- spontaneous bleeding
- easy bruising

A patient’s previous experience of bleeding in response to invasive dental or surgical procedures or to trauma may be a useful indicator of the likelihood of bleeding complications from the current dental treatment.

## 1.2 Managing Bleeding Risk

Guidance on the management of dental treatment for a patient taking an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug(s), based on the assessment of the bleeding risk, is presented in the form of general advice in this section, with specific advice for the different drug groups presented in later sections.

The management of intra- and post-operative bleeding is the responsibility of the primary care practitioner who provides the dental treatment, and they should take the appropriate steps to avoid bleeding complications.

Practitioners should however be aware of the local arrangements for access to emergency care for the very rare occasions where bleeding cannot be controlled in the primary care setting.


### 1.2.1 Haemostatic Measures

The arrest of bleeding is a core skill for primary dental care and the dental practitioner should have the necessary equipment and skills to perform appropriate local haemostatic measures competently for dental procedures likely to cause bleeding.

These include packing any open sockets with haemostatic material and placing sutures. Suturing may be used to stabilise the clot, packing material and wound margins, unless it is likely to cause further trauma.

For all patients taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs, haemostasis should be achieved using local measures prior to the patient being discharged from care.

Serious consideration should be given to suturing and packing, taking into account all relevant patient factors. These may include:

- the drug or drug combination that the patient is taking;
- other medical conditions that may impact on bleeding;
- other medications that may impact on bleeding; and
- the travel time for the patient to access emergency care if required.

Failure of initial haemostasis will necessitate packing and suturing at a later time.

Patients taking aspirin alone are unlikely to have a higher risk of post-operative bleeding complications than non-anticoagulated patients and may not require suturing.

The dental practitioner should have available:

- Absorbent gauze
- Haemostatic packing material, for example oxidized cellulose or collagen sponge
- Suture kit, including:
  - needle holders
  - tissue forceps
  - suture material
  - scissors

Some of these materials contain animal-based protein, which may not be acceptable to all patients for ethical, cultural or religious reasons.

Practices should ensure that non-animal-based products are also available and establish patient preferences before treatment.

Some guidelines recommend the use of tranexamic acid (TXA) mouthwash as an additional haemostatic measure.

While there is evidence that TXA reduces the bleeding risk from dental treatment in patients on antithrombotic therapies when compared to placebo, it may not have significant benefit when compared to other haemostatic measures.

Tranexamic acid is not included in the List of Dental Preparations in the British National Formulary (BNF) and therefore cannot be prescribed on the NHS.

In addition, tranexamic acid is not available as a mouthwash and therefore has to be prepared and prescribed "off label".

Based on these considerations, this guidance does not advise primary care practitioners to prescribe tranexamic acid for dental procedures.

However, if tranexamic acid is prescribed by the patient’s medical practitioner, then it should be used in addition to local measures.


### 1.2.2 Management of Patients in Remote and Rural Locations

Patients living in remote and rural locations may have to travel for longer to access primary care dental treatment, or secondary care in those very rare circumstances when a severe bleeding complication occurs, and may have difficulty accessing healthcare professionals out of hours.

The individual circumstances should be taken into consideration for patients in remote and rural settings and particular emphasis should be placed on the use of measures to avoid complications.

These may include:

- limiting the initial treatment area;
- staging treatment;
- use of haemostatic measures; and
- extended post-operative monitoring prior to discharge.

As with all patients, attitude to risk and the consequences of bleeding complications should be discussed and given due consideration when agreeing treatment.


### 1.2.3 Contacts and Referrals

If there is a lack of clarity regarding a patient’s medication or their medical condition, in order to assess their bleeding risk and to inform treatment planning, consult with the patient’s prescribing clinician.

This might be the haematologist or cardiologist rather than the patient’s general medical practitioner and should be done in advance of dental treatment, with interim, non-invasive measures used to relieve pain if required.

By following the recommendations in this guidance, dentists should be able to safely treat the vast majority of patients in primary care.

If necessary, colleagues in primary or secondary dental care could be consulted for advice on aspects of the procedure.

This may include:

- a more experienced senior colleague in practice;
- a speciality dentist or dentist with enhanced skills;
- a senior dental officer, for example in special care dentistry or oral surgery; or
- exceptionally, in very complex cases, a consultant or specialist in primary or secondary dental care.

It is likely to be easier to contact a colleague in your own setting than in another dental service.

If specialist advice from the hospital dental service is sought, it may take time to receive a reply from a suitably senior member of the hospital dental team.

For exceptional cases, if there is concern about whether a patient can be treated safely in primary care, contact a colleague in secondary care to discuss the most appropriate management for the patient before any referral is made.

This will help avoid unnecessary or inappropriate referral and ensure that the patient is referred to the most suitable service.

If referring a patient, details of the patient’s anticoagulation medication should be included in the referral.


### 1.2.4 General Advice for Managing Bleeding Risk

The following best practice advice is based on clinical experience and expert opinion.

#### 1.2.4.1 Dental Treatment That Is Unlikely to Cause Bleeding

For a patient who is taking an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug(s) and requires dental treatment that is unlikely to cause bleeding:

Treat the patient following standard procedures, taking care to avoid causing bleeding.


#### 1.2.4.2 Dental Treatment That Is Likely to Cause Bleeding

For a patient who is taking an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug(s), and requires dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with either a low or higher risk of bleeding complications:

If the patient is on a time-limited course of anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication, delay non-urgent, invasive dental procedures where possible until the medication has been discontinued.

- If the medication is being taken in preparation for an elective surgical procedure, it may be possible, in a dental emergency, to interrupt the drug treatment in liaison with the surgical consultant.
- Patients with acute deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism may be taking high-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban for the first 1 to 3 weeks of treatment. It would be advisable to delay any dental procedures likely to cause bleeding until the patient is taking the standard dose.

If the patient has another relevant medical condition(s) or is taking other medications that may increase bleeding risk, consult with the patient’s prescribing clinician, specialist or general medical practitioner if more information is required in order to assess the likely impact on bleeding risk.

- Consulting in advance of the appointment could reduce wasted appointments and travel.

If advice is required on aspects of the procedure, liaise with a more experienced colleague, ideally in your own setting.

- Clinical experience indicates that most patients can be safely treated in primary care and only in exceptional circumstances should referral be required.
- Enquiries about the patient’s medication should be directed to the prescribing clinician.

Plan treatment for early in the day and week, where possible, to allow time for the management of prolonged bleeding or rebleeding episodes, should they occur.

Provide the patient with pre-treatment instructions, for example for timing of INR testing or any modification of their medication schedule.

- Provision of pre-treatment instructions, for example electronically or in writing, could reduce wasted appointments and travel.

Perform the procedure as atraumatically as possible, use appropriate local measures and only discharge the patient once haemostasis has been achieved.

- Suturing and packing at the time of treatment may reduce the likelihood of the patient having to reattend or travel to emergency care for the management of post-operative bleeding.

If travel time to emergency care is a concern, place particular emphasis at the time of the initial treatment on the use of measures to avoid complications.

These may include:

- limiting the initial treatment area;
- staging treatment;
- haemostatic measures; and
- post-treatment monitoring.

Advise the patient to take paracetamol, unless contraindicated, for pain relief rather than NSAIDs such as:

- aspirin;
- ibuprofen;
- diclofenac; or
- naproxen.

Provide the patient with post-treatment advice and emergency contact details.

Follow the specific recommendations and advice given in the later sections for the management of patients taking the different anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs.

Do not interrupt anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, except under direct written instruction from the patient’s cardiologist, for:

- patients with prosthetic metal heart valves or coronary stents;
- patients who have had a pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis in the last three months; or
- patients on anticoagulant therapy for cardioversion.

## 1.3 Treating a Patient Taking a Direct Oral Anticoagulant

Quantitative laboratory tests for assessing coagulation levels in patients taking direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are not widely available and the INR test used for warfarin is not suitable for these drugs.

However, since the DOACs generally provide more predictable anticoagulation, monitoring is considered less important than for warfarin and is not carried out routinely.

Compared to warfarin, the DOACs exhibit a rapid onset of action, generally 1 to 4 hours, and have relatively short half-lives:

- rivaroxaban: approximately 5 to 13 hours;
- apixaban: approximately 12 hours;
- edoxaban: approximately 10 to 14 hours; and
- dabigatran: approximately 13 to 18 hours, depending on renal function and age.

Because of these pharmacokinetic properties, it is possible to modify an individual’s anticoagulation status relatively rapidly, minimising the period in which anticoagulation activity is therapeutically sub-optimal.

In the event of severe bleeding, the short half-lives of these drugs allow for a relatively rapid reduction of their anticoagulant effects.

Specific reversal agents for dabigatran, apixaban and rivaroxaban are available for use in hospital settings for life-threatening bleeding.

Apixaban and dabigatran are taken twice a day, while rivaroxaban and edoxaban are most commonly taken once a day, either in the morning or at night.

For each of these drugs, a lower dose may be indicated for certain patients, including those with various levels of renal impairment and, in some cases, older people.

Patients with acute deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism may be taking high-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban for the first 1 to 3 weeks of treatment.

It would be advisable to delay any dental procedures likely to cause bleeding until the patient is taking the standard dose.


### 1.3.1 Key Recommendations

For a patient who is taking a DOAC and requires a dental procedure with a low risk of bleeding complications:

Treat without interrupting their anticoagulant medication.

**Conditional recommendation; very low certainty evidence.**

For a patient who is taking a DOAC and requires a dental procedure with a higher risk of bleeding complications:

Advise them to:

- miss their morning dose if taking apixaban or dabigatran; or
- delay their morning dose if taking rivaroxaban or edoxaban

on the day of their dental treatment.

**Conditional recommendation; very low certainty evidence.**

These recommendations apply to patients taking:

- apixaban;
- dabigatran;
- rivaroxaban; or
- edoxaban.

There is a growing body of direct evidence and clinical experience relating to invasive dental treatment for patients taking a DOAC.

The recommendations are based on:

- the available evidence;
- the balance of likely effects of each option for each dental procedure;
- the known characteristics of the drugs, including their short half-lives and rapid onset of action; and
- expert consensus.

Recent evidence suggests that while there may be a higher risk of post-operative bleeding complications for patients taking DOACs compared with patients taking no anticoagulant, there might not be a significant difference when comparing continuation versus interruption of DOAC therapy for a range of dental procedures.

Reported bleeding events were manageable with local haemostatic measures.

Much of the available evidence relates to extractions, and there is limited evidence relating to more invasive procedures.

The evidence is considered to be of very low certainty because of study limitations.

These are conditional recommendations because of the limited evidence and the fine balance between the potential risks and benefits of the treatment options.

The estimated risk to the patient of a thromboembolic event resulting from brief DOAC interruption is judged to be extremely small.

The risk of a bleeding complication if the DOAC is continued is also likely to be small but depends on:

- the procedure involved; and
- the individual patient.

The potential risks from either continuing or interrupting a patient’s DOAC medication are uncertain.

For a patient who is taking a DOAC and requires dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with either a low or higher risk of bleeding complications:

Discuss with the patient the possible benefits and harms and the balance of risks for continuing or interrupting their DOAC medication in the context of the required dental treatment when gaining consent.

Since the advice on interruption of medication is for a patient to miss at most a single dose of the DOAC, or to delay it for several hours, it is not usually necessary to consult with the patient’s medical practitioner first.

However, if the dental practitioner or patient has concerns, these should be discussed with the patient’s:

- prescribing clinician;
- specialist; or
- general medical practitioner.

Patients waiting for cardioversion can require at least 3 weeks of uninterrupted anticoagulation prior to the procedure.

Their anticoagulation therapy should not be modified.


### 1.3.2 Management for Procedures with a Low Risk of Bleeding Complications

The available evidence suggests that it is not necessary to interrupt DOAC medication for dental procedures that are likely to cause bleeding but have a low risk of bleeding complications.

Because the bleeding risk for these procedures is likely to be low, the balance of effects is in favour of continuing DOAC treatment without modification to avoid increasing the risk of a thromboembolic event.

Anticoagulant therapy is prescribed for significant clinical indications and should not be interrupted unnecessarily.

Although treating a patient in the morning is more likely to coincide with the relative peak of drug concentration, this risk is judged to be outweighed by the importance of being able to manage a bleeding complication, should one occur, during surgery hours.

For a patient who is taking a DOAC and requires dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with a low risk of bleeding complications:

Treat the patient according to the general advice for managing bleeding risk without advising the patient to miss or delay a dose of their medication.

In addition:

- Plan treatment for early in the day to allow for monitoring and management of bleeding complications, should they occur.
- Limit the initial treatment area, for example:
  - perform a single extraction; or
  - limit root surface debridement to three teeth and assess bleeding before continuing.
- Use local haemostatic measures to achieve haemostasis.
- Strongly consider suturing and packing, taking into account all relevant patient factors.


### 1.3.3 Management for Procedures with a Higher Risk of Bleeding Complications

There is limited evidence on the risk of bleeding complications for more invasive dental procedures in patients taking DOACs.

The consensus of expert opinion is that patients should be advised to:

- miss a dose of apixaban or dabigatran; or
- delay a dose of rivaroxaban or edoxaban

prior to dental procedures that are likely to cause bleeding and have a higher risk of bleeding complications.

Because the risk of bleeding complications for these procedures is considered to be higher, the balance of effects is in favour of missing or delaying the pre-treatment DOAC dose.

Due to the short half-lives of the DOACs, this will significantly reduce the level of anticoagulation at the time of dental treatment.

The brief interruption and rapid onset of action of the DOAC when restarting reduces the period during which the patient may have sub-therapeutic anticoagulation and minimises the effect on thromboembolic risk.

For a patient who is taking a DOAC and requires dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with a higher risk of bleeding complications:

Advise the patient to:

- miss the morning dose of apixaban or dabigatran; or
- delay the morning dose of rivaroxaban or edoxaban

on the day of their dental treatment.

Treat the patient according to the general advice for managing bleeding risk.

If the patient usually takes their once-a-day rivaroxaban or edoxaban in the evening, there is no need to modify their medication schedule before the dental treatment.

In addition:

- Plan treatment for early in the day to allow for monitoring and management of bleeding complications, should they occur.
- Consider carrying out treatments in a staged manner, where possible, over separate visits.
- Use local haemostatic measures to achieve haemostasis.
- Strongly consider suturing and packing, taking into account all relevant patient factors.
- Advise the patient when to restart their medication.


#### 1.3.3.1 Restarting Rivaroxaban or Edoxaban

For rivaroxaban or edoxaban taken once a day:

- A delayed morning dose may be taken 4 hours after haemostasis has been achieved.
- The next dose should be taken as usual the following morning.

If the patient normally takes rivaroxaban or edoxaban in the evening:

- They can take the medication at the usual time on the day of treatment;
- however, it should be taken no earlier than 4 hours after haemostasis has been achieved.


#### 1.3.3.2 Restarting Apixaban or Dabigatran

For apixaban or dabigatran taken twice a day:

- after missing the morning dose, the patient should take their evening dose at the usual time;
- however, the dose should be taken no earlier than 4 hours after haemostasis has been achieved.


#### 1.3.3.3 Bleeding After Restarting the DOAC

Advise the patient to contact the dental practice for advice if bleeding occurs before or after restarting their DOAC.

The patient should avoid missing subsequent doses of their DOAC unless this is absolutely required in an emergency situation to control bleeding.


#### 1.3.3.4 Urgent Higher-Risk Dental Treatment

Many higher-risk dental procedures are likely to be elective.

However, there may be rare occasions when a higher-risk procedure is required urgently.

If the patient has already taken their morning dose of DOAC, it is advisable to delay the procedure until later in the day, where possible, to allow anticoagulation levels to decrease.

## 1.4 Treating a Patient Taking Warfarin or Another Vitamin K Antagonist

Although the use of warfarin is well established, managing its therapeutic anticoagulation activity can be complicated.

Due to substantial drug and dietary interactions, variation in patients’ responses to the drug and its narrow therapeutic range, warfarin activity has to be monitored frequently.

This is achieved using the INR (International Normalised Ratio) test, which measures the time taken for a clot to form in a blood sample, relative to a standard.

An INR value of 1 indicates a level of coagulation equivalent to that of an average patient not taking warfarin, and values greater than 1 indicate a longer clotting time and thus a longer bleeding time.

The INR test is also used for patients taking the less commonly used vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), acenocoumarol and phenindione.

Target INR levels differ depending on the indication for which the drug is prescribed and can range from 2.5-3.5 ± 0.5.

A patient’s warfarin therapy will be adjusted by their medical practitioner or anticoagulation service, or by the patient if self-monitoring, as necessary to achieve the target INR level appropriate for their medical condition.

Patients taking warfarin will have a record of their INR test results, which they should present when attending for dental treatment.


### 1.4.1 Key Recommendation

For a patient who is taking warfarin or another vitamin K antagonist, with an INR below 4, treat without interrupting their anticoagulant medication.

**Strong recommendation; low certainty evidence.**

This recommendation is based on the available evidence and extensive clinical experience.

Low certainty evidence from several systematic reviews suggests that although the bleeding risk for dental procedures is likely to be higher in patients on vitamin K antagonist therapy than in non-anticoagulated patients, there might not be any significant difference for patients continuing versus interrupting their vitamin K antagonist.

Overall, the bleeding risk is low and in the vast majority of cases, bleeding events can be controlled with local haemostatic measures.

Although the evidence on bleeding risk is of low certainty, this is considered to be a strong recommendation because of emphasis placed on the potential risk of a serious thromboembolic event if warfarin treatment is interrupted.

Due to the longer half-life of warfarin, interrupting therapy would potentially leave the patient at sub-therapeutic levels of anticoagulation for longer than interruption of a direct oral anticoagulant.


### 1.4.2 Dental Treatment That Is Likely to Cause Bleeding

For dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with either a low or higher risk of bleeding complications:

Ensure that the patient’s INR has been checked, ideally no more than 24 hours before the procedure.

If the patient has a stable INR, checking the INR no more than 72 hours before the procedure is acceptable.

A stable patient is one who:

- does not require weekly monitoring; and
- has not had any INR measurements above 4 in the last two months.

If there is reason to believe that a test result obtained up to 72 hours before dental treatment may not reflect the current level, the patient’s INR should be tested again no more than 24 hours before the dental procedure.


### 1.4.3 INR 4 or Above

If the patient’s INR is 4 or above:

- Inform the patient’s anticoagulation service or general medical practitioner.
- Delay invasive dental treatment until the patient’s INR has been reduced to less than 4.
- For urgent treatment, refer the patient to secondary dental care.

If the patient is prescribed an antibiotic for the dental condition, be aware that this might affect their INR level.


### 1.4.4 INR Below 4

If the patient’s INR is below 4:

Treat according to the general advice for managing bleeding risk without interrupting their anticoagulant.

In addition:

- Consider limiting the initial treatment area.
- For example, perform a single extraction or limit root surface debridement to three teeth, then assess bleeding before continuing.
- For procedures with a higher risk of post-operative bleeding complications, consider carrying out the treatments in a staged manner, where possible, over separate visits.
- Use local haemostatic measures to achieve haemostasis.
- Strongly consider suturing and packing, taking into account all relevant patient factors.

## 1.5 Treating a Patient Taking an Injectable Anticoagulant

The low molecular weight heparins (LMWHs), dalteparin, enoxaparin and tinzaparin, are used for the prophylaxis and treatment of venous thromboembolism and are administered parenterally by subcutaneous injection rather than orally as for the other anticoagulants discussed.

Although used in limited patient groups, these may still be encountered in primary or secondary dental care settings.

Patients taking LMWHs include:

- pregnant women with indications for anticoagulation;
- patients with venous thrombosis with a background of cancer;
- patients taking LMWHs for short-term use following orthopaedic or other surgery; and
- care home residents with limited mobility and other risk factors who may be taking LMWHs for thromboprophylaxis.

LMWHs may be administered once or twice a day at either prophylactic or treatment (therapeutic) doses.

Lower doses are generally used for prophylaxis whereas treatment doses tend to be higher.

However, the doses can vary for individuals depending on weight and renal function and may be further adjusted by the patient’s specialist, based on measuring anti-Xa levels, to non-standard unlicensed doses for a given indication.

Consequently, there can be overlap between doses used for prophylaxis or treatment and advice from the prescriber of the patient’s LMWH might need to be sought to confirm the type of dose being taken.


### 1.5.1 Licensed Prophylactic and Treatment Doses of LMWHs

#### Dalteparin

**Prophylactic (low) dose:**

- 2,500-5,000 units once daily.

**Treatment (higher) dose:**

- 7,500-18,000 units once daily; or
- 5,000-10,000 units twice daily.

In a 70 kg adult, a treatment dose may be expected to be approximately 15,000 units once daily.


#### Enoxaparin

**Prophylactic (low) dose:**

- 2,000-4,000 units once daily;
- equivalent to 20-40 mg.

**Treatment (higher) dose:**

- 150 units/kg (1.5 mg/kg) once daily; or
- 100 units/kg (1 mg/kg) twice daily.

In a 70 kg adult, a treatment dose may be expected to be:

- approximately 10,500 units (105 mg) once daily; or
- 7,000 units (70 mg) twice daily.


#### Tinzaparin

**Prophylactic (low) dose:**

- 3,500-4,500 units once daily.

**Treatment (higher) dose:**

- 175 units/kg once daily.

In a 70 kg adult, a treatment dose may be expected to be approximately 12,250 units once daily.

Doses may be adjusted in patients with renal impairment or in patients with body weight below 50 kg or above 100 kg.


### 1.5.2 Key Recommendation

For a patient who is taking a prophylactic (low) dose of a low molecular weight heparin, treat without interrupting their anticoagulant medication.

**Conditional recommendation; very low certainty evidence.**

There is a lack of direct clinical evidence regarding the risk of bleeding complications for dental patients taking injectable anticoagulants, including the LMWHs.

Treatment doses of LMWHs are generally considered to be equivalent to treatment with warfarin within the therapeutic range or with direct oral anticoagulants.

Indirect non-dental evidence suggests that there may be no significant difference in the risk of bleeding events in patients having long-term treatment for venous thromboembolism when comparing LMWHs with vitamin K antagonists.

The risk of bleeding for patients taking LMWHs is dose dependent.

Therefore, for patients taking a prophylactic (low) dose, the risk is likely to be lower than for a treatment dose and may be lower than for patients taking a vitamin K antagonist.

This recommendation applies only to patients taking low prophylactic doses where the bleeding risk is likely to be lowest.

It is a conditional recommendation because of the uncertainty about bleeding risk for patients taking LMWHs who require dental treatment.

For patients taking treatment doses, consultation with the patient’s prescribing clinician or specialist would usually be required to assess the likely impact of their medication and medical condition on their bleeding risk and establish the appropriate dental treatment management.


### 1.5.3 Management for Dental Treatment Likely to Cause Bleeding

For a patient who is taking a LMWH and requires dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with either a low or higher risk of bleeding complications:

Establish whether the patient is taking:

- a prophylactic (low) dose; or
- a treatment (higher) dose.


#### 1.5.3.1 Patients with Renal Impairment or Extreme Body Weight

For patients with renal impairment, or body weight below 50 kg or above 100 kg, the dose of LMWH is likely to have been adjusted.

Consultation with the prescribing clinician, such as the specialist or general medical practitioner, may be required to confirm whether the patient is taking a prophylactic or treatment dose.


#### 1.5.3.2 Treatment or Higher Dose

If the patient is taking a treatment (higher) dose, or there is uncertainty about the dose they are taking:

Consult with the prescribing clinician, such as the specialist or general medical practitioner, in order to assess the likely impact on bleeding risk for the dental procedure.


#### 1.5.3.3 Prophylactic or Low Dose

If the patient is taking a prophylactic (low) dose:

Treat according to the general advice for managing bleeding risk without interrupting their LMWH.

In addition:

- Consider limiting the initial treatment area.
- For example, perform a single extraction or limit root surface debridement to three teeth, then assess bleeding before continuing.
- For procedures with a higher risk of post-operative bleeding complications, consider carrying out the treatments in a staged manner, where possible, over separate visits.
- Use local haemostatic measures to achieve haemostasis.
- Strongly consider suturing and packing, taking into account all relevant patient factors.


### 1.5.4 Short-Term LMWH Therapy

LMWHs are prescribed for relatively short periods of time following elective orthopaedic surgery, up to 6 weeks.

Where possible, dental treatment likely to cause bleeding should be delayed for these patients until they are no longer taking anticoagulants.


### 1.5.5 Dental Treatment After Stopping LMWH

Like the DOACs, the LMWHs have a short onset of action and short half-lives.

In relation to major surgery, typical advice is to interrupt LMWHs 24 hours before treatment.

It would therefore be reasonable to assume that dental treatment could proceed for a patient who has recently stopped taking a LMWH 24 hours or more previously, unless there are any other concerns.


### 1.5.6 Patients Receiving Heparin During Kidney Dialysis

For patients given heparin or one of the LMWHs during kidney dialysis, where possible dental treatments likely to cause bleeding should be delayed until the following day.

## 1.6 Treating a Patient Taking an Antiplatelet Drug(s)

Patients taking antiplatelet medications tend to have prolonged bleeding times, which is a consequence of the requirement for platelet aggregation in the formation of the initial platelet plug in primary haemostasis.

This should be taken into consideration when planning dental treatments likely to cause bleeding, to ensure that sufficient time is available to achieve and monitor haemostasis.

There is no suitable test equivalent to the INR for measuring the antiplatelet effect of the various drugs that patients may be taking.

Patients on dual antiplatelet therapies may have a higher risk of prolonged bleeding compared to those on a single antiplatelet drug and should be managed accordingly.

The most commonly encountered antiplatelet combination is aspirin with clopidogrel for acute coronary syndrome.

Dipyridamole with aspirin after a stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA) is less commonly prescribed, as clopidogrel monotherapy is considered to be more effective and better tolerated.

The newer antiplatelet drugs prasugrel and ticagrelor are only prescribed in combination with aspirin and are only licensed for patients with acute coronary syndrome.

Although evidence relating to bleeding risks with prasugrel and ticagrelor in the context of dental procedures is very limited, the risk of surgical bleeding complications is considered to be higher for prasugrel or ticagrelor compared to clopidogrel.

Discontinuation of single or dual antiplatelet therapy has been associated with an increased risk of adverse thromboembolic events.

Patients with a coronary artery stent will be prescribed dual antiplatelet therapy for up to 12 months.

It is extremely important that this treatment is not stopped prematurely or interrupted without prior discussion and written advice from the patient’s cardiologist because of the risk of major adverse cardiac events.


### 1.6.1 Key Recommendation

For a patient who is taking single or dual antiplatelet drugs, treat without interrupting their antiplatelet medication.

**Strong recommendation; low certainty evidence.**

This recommendation is based on the available evidence and extensive clinical experience.

The risk of post-operative bleeding is likely to be higher for dental patients on dual antiplatelet therapy than for those on single antiplatelet therapy or none.

However, the reported incidence of bleeding complications is low, ranging from approximately 0.4% to 3.8% across studies, with events controllable using local haemostatic measures.

Although the evidence on bleeding risk is of low certainty, this is considered a strong recommendation because of the potential risk of a serious adverse thromboembolic event if antiplatelet treatment is interrupted.


### 1.6.2 Dental Treatment That Is Likely to Cause Bleeding

For dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with either a low or higher risk of bleeding complications:

Treat the patient according to the general advice for managing bleeding risk without interrupting their antiplatelet medication.


### 1.6.3 Patient Taking Aspirin Alone

If the patient is taking aspirin alone:

- Consider limiting the initial treatment area.
- For example, perform a single extraction or limit root surface debridement to three teeth, then assess bleeding before continuing.
- For procedures with a higher risk of post-operative bleeding complications, consider carrying out the treatments in a staged manner, where possible, over separate visits.
- Use local haemostatic measures to achieve haemostasis.


### 1.6.4 Patient Taking Another Single Antiplatelet Drug or Dual Antiplatelet Drugs

If the patient is taking another single antiplatelet drug or dual antiplatelet drugs:

Be aware that bleeding may be prolonged for up to an hour.

This should be taken into account when planning treatment time.

In addition:

- Limit the initial treatment area.
- For example, perform a single extraction or limit root surface debridement to three teeth, then assess bleeding before continuing.
- For procedures with a higher risk of post-operative bleeding complications, consider carrying out the treatments in a staged manner, where possible, over separate visits.
- Use local haemostatic measures to achieve haemostasis.
- Strongly consider suturing and packing, taking into account all relevant patient factors.

## 1.7 Treating a Patient Taking an Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Drug Combination

For some patients, combinations of anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications are prescribed, including a DOAC or warfarin with aspirin or clopidogrel, or in rare cases, triple drug combinations.

Patients with atrial fibrillation and recent myocardial infarction may be taking a DOAC with dual antiplatelet therapy.

Because of the conditions for which combination therapies are prescribed, including coronary stents, the patient’s medication should not be stopped prematurely or interrupted without prior discussion and written advice from the patient’s cardiologist.

Patients on combination therapies are likely to have a higher bleeding risk and may have additional medical complications.


### 1.7.1 Dental Treatment That Is Likely to Cause Bleeding

For a patient who is taking an anticoagulant and antiplatelet drug combination and requires dental treatment that is likely to cause bleeding, with either a low or higher risk of bleeding complications:

Consult with the patient’s prescribing clinician in order to assess the likely impact of the particular drug combination and the patient’s medical condition on their bleeding risk.

Some patients might only be on these drug combinations for 3 to 4 weeks, so it may be possible to delay dental treatment.

## 1.8 Drug Interactions Between Anticoagulants or Antiplatelet Drugs and Other Medications

There are a large number of documented interactions between anticoagulants or antiplatelet medications and other prescription drugs.

For complete listings, consult the British National Formulary (BNF) or the individual drug Summary of Product Characteristics (SPC) sheets.

For the purposes of this guidance, only the interactions between anticoagulants or antiplatelet medications and drugs that are available in the BNF Dental Practitioner’s Formulary are considered.

These interactions are listed in Appendix 4 of the original SDCEP guideline.

Advice on management can be found in the BNF and SDCEP Drug Prescribing for Dentistry guidance.

When prescribing drugs to patients who are taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents, be aware of potential interactions that might affect coagulation levels.

## 1.9 Interactions with Drugs Prescribed by Dentists

This section shows possible interactions and effects between anticoagulants or antiplatelet medications and drugs prescribed by dentists.

The information summarises the main interactions and is not exhaustive. The information is subject to change, especially for the newer drugs.

For further information and advice about interactions, refer to the British National Formulary (BNF), the individual drug Summary of Product Characteristics (SPCs), and SDCEP Drug Prescribing for Dentistry guidance.

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.
- Clarithromycin, erythromycin and azithromycin may increase bleeding risk.
- Carbamazepine may reduce the plasma concentration of dabigatran.


#### 1.9.1.3 Rivaroxaban

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.
- Clarithromycin and erythromycin may increase bleeding risk.
- Carbamazepine may reduce the plasma concentration of rivaroxaban.


#### 1.9.1.4 Edoxaban

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.
- Clarithromycin, erythromycin and azithromycin may increase bleeding risk.
- Carbamazepine may reduce the plasma concentration of edoxaban.


### 1.9.2 Vitamin K Antagonists

#### 1.9.2.1 Warfarin, Phenindione and Acenocoumarol

Possible interactions include:

- Metronidazole can notably increase the anticoagulant effect.
- Other antibiotics, including penicillins, macrolides, clindamycin and tetracyclines, may enhance the anticoagulant effect in a minority of patients.
- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.
- Carbamazepine may reduce the anticoagulant effect.
- Miconazole and fluconazole may cause an established and clinically important increase in anticoagulation effect.

Fever or infection can affect coagulation or drug metabolism. Therefore, a patient who is systemically unwell enough to require an antibiotic may have an altered coagulation status.

The expectation of an interaction with a vitamin K antagonist should not exclude the use of an antibiotic if it is considered clinically appropriate.

If antibiotics are prescribed:

- advise the patient that their INR might be increased; and
- inform the patient’s general practitioner or anticoagulation service so that increased INR monitoring can be considered.

Miconazole should be avoided in patients taking warfarin or acenocoumarol.


### 1.9.3 Injectable Anticoagulants

#### 1.9.3.1 Dalteparin, Enoxaparin and Tinzaparin

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.


### 1.9.4 Antiplatelet Drugs

#### 1.9.4.1 Aspirin

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and diclofenac may increase bleeding risk.
- Regular use of ibuprofen may reduce the antiplatelet effect of aspirin.


#### 1.9.4.2 Clopidogrel

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.
- Erythromycin may reduce the antiplatelet effect.
- Fluconazole may reduce the antiplatelet effect.
- Omeprazole may reduce the antiplatelet effect.

Fluconazole or omeprazole should be avoided in patients taking clopidogrel.


#### 1.9.4.3 Dipyridamole

Possible interactions include:

- Aspirin may increase bleeding risk.


#### 1.9.4.4 Prasugrel

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.


#### 1.9.4.5 Ticagrelor

Possible interactions include:

- NSAIDs, including aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, may increase bleeding risk.
- Clarithromycin and azithromycin may increase the plasma concentration of ticagrelor.
- Carbamazepine may reduce the plasma concentration of ticagrelor.

Clarithromycin should be avoided in patients taking ticagrelor.


### 1.9.5 Additional Advice on NSAIDs

The use of NSAIDs is discouraged in patients with vascular disease because of their antiplatelet action.

Simple analgesics such as paracetamol or co-codamol should be tried first.

If an NSAID is required:

- treatment length should be kept to a minimum; and
- gastroprotection should be considered.


### 1.9.6 Additional Advice on Carbamazepine

Carbamazepine should be avoided in patients taking:

- ticagrelor;
- apixaban;
- dabigatran; or
- rivaroxaban.

Carbamazepine should be used with caution in patients taking edoxaban or avoided if possible.


### 1.9.7 Additional Advice on Macrolide Antibiotics and DOACs

There is a risk of interaction between any DOAC and a macrolide antibiotic, including:

- clarithromycin;
- erythromycin; and
- azithromycin.

The clinical significance of these interactions may vary.

If erythromycin is prescribed for a patient taking edoxaban, liaison with the patient’s prescriber is required for edoxaban dose reduction.

## 2.1 Key Points

An increasing number of patients are prescribed anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, and the number of medications available for these purposes has also increased.

There is strong evidence for the older medications, including warfarin and antiplatelet agents, and more limited evidence for the newer direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs). For most patients, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy does not need to be altered before dental intervention.

### 2.1.1 Typical Patient

For the typical patient:

- There is no need to discontinue anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication.
- Local measures should be used to control bleeding.

### 2.1.2 Patients with Higher Risk of Bleeding

For patients with a higher risk of bleeding, any suggested modification to the medication regimen before dental surgery should be made in consultation with and on the advice of the patient's physician.


## 2.2 Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Medications

### 2.2.1 Anticoagulant

- warfarin (Coumadin)

The ADA describes the evidence supporting dental management with warfarin as strong.


### 2.2.2 Antiplatelet Agents

- clopidogrel (Plavix)
- ticlopidine (Ticlid)
- prasugrel (Effient)
- ticagrelor (Brilinta)
- aspirin

The ADA describes the evidence supporting dental management with these older antiplatelet medications as strong.


### 2.2.3 Direct-Acting Oral Anticoagulants

- dabigatran (Pradaxa)
- rivaroxaban (Xarelto)
- apixaban (Eliquis)
- edoxaban (Savaysa; marketed as Lixiana in Europe, Japan, and some other countries)

The ADA describes the available evidence for these newer direct-acting oral anticoagulants as limited.


## 2.3 Introduction

Oral anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications are prescribed for patients who are at high risk for, or who have previously experienced, thromboembolic events.

Examples include patients with:

- deep-vein thrombosis (DVT)
- pulmonary embolism (PE)
- nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (NVAF)

Nonvalvular atrial fibrillation is a cardiac arrhythmia that predisposes patients to blood clot formation.

Oral anticoagulants include the vitamin K antagonist warfarin and the newer direct-acting oral anticoagulants.

Among the direct-acting oral anticoagulants:

- dabigatran is a direct thrombin inhibitor;
- apixaban is a factor Xa inhibitor;
- rivaroxaban is a factor Xa inhibitor; and
- edoxaban is a factor Xa inhibitor.

Oral antiplatelet medications include:

- clopidogrel
- ticlopidine
- prasugrel
- ticagrelor
- aspirin

Adverse effects associated with anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy can include prolonged bleeding and bruising.

Without anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, patients who require these medications may have an increased risk of blood clot formation that could result in:

- thromboembolism
- stroke
- myocardial infarction

The serious risks associated with stopping or reducing anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy must therefore be balanced against the potential consequences of prolonged bleeding following dental treatment.

Dental bleeding can generally be controlled using local measures such as:

- mechanical pressure
- hemostatic agents, including absorbable gelatin or oxidized cellulose
- suturing
- antifibrinolytic agents such as tranexamic acid


## 2.4 Evidence: Direct-Acting Oral Anticoagulants

Four direct-acting oral anticoagulants are approved for use in the United States for indications that include prevention or treatment of deep-vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, or reduction of the risk of stroke and systemic embolism in patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation:

- dabigatran
- apixaban
- rivaroxaban
- edoxaban

Compared with traditional oral anticoagulant therapy such as warfarin, these agents:

- act on specific targets in the coagulation pathway;
- are administered in fixed doses;
- have more predictable pharmacokinetics;
- have shorter half-lives;
- require little or no routine laboratory monitoring; and
- have fewer drug and food interactions.

There is no direct prospective-trial evidence comparing different peri-procedural management strategies for dental patients taking direct-acting oral anticoagulants and evaluating patient outcomes.

However, based on the available limited evidence, in most cases there is no need to alter the anticoagulant regimen before most dental interventions.


### 2.4.1 Dental Interventions That May Not Require DOAC Discontinuation

A 2015 consensus guideline from the European Heart Rhythm Association, with a later executive summary, identified several interventions that do not necessarily require discontinuation of newer oral anticoagulants.

These include:

- extraction of 1 to 3 teeth
- periodontal surgery
- incision of an abscess
- dental implant placement

These interventions were identified in the EHRA guidance and its subsequent executive summary. [3][4]

### 2.4.2 Continuing Versus Discontinuing DOAC Therapy

A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated management of direct oral anticoagulants for invasive oral procedures.

The review included 21 studies, but no randomized controlled trials were identified.

Six studies directly compared patients who continued DOAC therapy with patients whose DOAC therapy was discontinued before dental procedures.

The review found no important difference in postoperative bleeding between patients who continued and those who discontinued direct oral anticoagulant therapy.

However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because the available evidence was of low quality and included relatively small numbers of patients. [5]


### 2.4.3 Low-to-Moderate Bleeding-Risk Dental Procedures

A 2018 systematic review examined management of patients taking direct-acting oral anticoagulants who required dental procedures associated with low-to-moderate bleeding risk.

Procedures classified as low bleeding risk included:

- administration of local anesthetic
- simple restorative procedures
- supragingival scaling
- single-tooth extraction

Procedures classified as moderate bleeding risk included:

- extraction of 2 to 4 teeth
- localized gingival surgery involving 5 or fewer teeth

Five studies were included in the evidence review.

Among patients taking direct-acting oral anticoagulants and undergoing procedures with low-to-moderate bleeding risk, bleeding rates were low whether the anticoagulant was continued or temporarily held around the procedure.

When bleeding occurred, it was generally mild and could be controlled using local hemostatic measures. [6]


### 2.4.4 More Conservative Approaches

Two narrative reviews discussed more conservative management approaches that may be considered in patients with a higher risk of bleeding or when the planned procedure is associated with increased perioperative bleeding risk.

One suggested approach is maximizing the amount of time between the patient's last anticoagulant dose and the dental intervention. [7][8]

For patients considered to have a higher risk of bleeding, the ADA also notes that possible approaches may include:

- postponing the daily anticoagulant dose until after the dental procedure;
- scheduling the dental intervention as late as possible after the patient's most recent anticoagulant dose; or
- temporarily interrupting anticoagulant therapy for approximately 24 to 48 hours.

Any suggested modification to the patient's anticoagulant regimen should be made in consultation with and on the advice of the patient's physician. [2]


## 2.5 Evidence: Warfarin and Antiplatelet Agents

Warfarin and antiplatelet medications such as clopidogrel, ticlopidine, prasugrel, ticagrelor, and aspirin are commonly used in patients who:

- have experienced deep-vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism;
- have experienced myocardial infarction;
- have undergone cardiac stent placement; or
- have nonvalvular atrial fibrillation.

There is general agreement, supported by strong evidence, that treatment regimens involving these older anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications generally should not be altered before dental procedures.


### 2.5.1 Warfarin

A 2009 systematic review and meta-analysis found no increased risk of bleeding when regular warfarin doses were continued compared with discontinuing or modifying warfarin in patients undergoing single or multiple tooth extractions. [9]

The American Academy of Neurology has recommended that patients taking aspirin or warfarin for stroke prevention who undergo dental procedures continue taking their medications. [10]

A 2015 systematic review addressing dental extractions in patients taking warfarin concluded that patients whose International Normalized Ratio (INR) was within the therapeutic range of 3.0 or less could continue their regular warfarin regimen before extraction. [11]

A 2016 Clinical Practice Statement from the American Academy of Oral Medicine concluded that moderately invasive oral surgery, defined as uncomplicated tooth extraction, is safe at an INR of approximately 3.5. Some experts cited in that statement considered treatment safe at an INR of up to 4.0. [12]

A 2008 systematic review and meta-analysis found that although hemorrhage and thromboembolism risks were reduced at an INR range of 2 to 3, moderately higher INR values appeared to be safe and more effective than subtherapeutic anticoagulation. [13]


## 2.6 Single or Dual Antiplatelet Therapy

The American Heart Association, American College of Cardiology, Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions, American College of Surgeons, and American Dental Association published a consensus opinion addressing coronary stents and antiplatelet therapy.

The consensus states that healthcare professionals performing invasive or surgical procedures, including dentists, who are concerned about peri-procedural or postoperative bleeding should contact the patient's cardiologist before discontinuing antiplatelet medication.

Because antiplatelet therapy is important after coronary stent placement to reduce the risk of stent thrombosis, these medications should not be discontinued prematurely. [14]

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis found clinically similar bleeding rates after minor oral surgery among patients receiving dual antiplatelet therapy, single antiplatelet therapy, or no antiplatelet therapy. Bleeding that occurred was managed with local measures, and the authors concluded that interruption of dual antiplatelet therapy before minor oral surgery was not advised. [15]

A 2013 systematic review found no clinically significant increase in postoperative bleeding complications following invasive dental procedures among patients receiving either single or dual antiplatelet therapy. [16]

## 2.7 Other Patient Considerations

Some patients taking one or more anticoagulant medications may have additional medical conditions or may be receiving other treatments or medications that increase their risk of prolonged bleeding after dental treatment.

Examples include:

- liver impairment
- alcoholism
- kidney failure
- thrombocytopenia
- hemophilia
- other hematologic disorders
- cytotoxic medications such as cancer chemotherapy

In these situations, dental practitioners may wish to consult the patient's physician to determine whether dental care can safely be delivered in a primary care office.

Any suggested modification to the patient's medication regimen before dental surgery should be made in consultation with and on the advice of the patient's physician.


## 2.8 Summary

For most patients taking older anticoagulants such as warfarin or antiplatelet medications such as clopidogrel, ticlopidine, prasugrel, ticagrelor, or aspirin, treatment generally should not be altered before dental procedures.

The risks associated with stopping or reducing these medications, including thromboembolism, stroke, and myocardial infarction, generally outweigh the consequences of prolonged dental bleeding, which can usually be controlled using local measures.

For patients with comorbid medical conditions or other therapies that increase the risk of prolonged bleeding, the dentist may wish to consult the patient's physician to determine whether treatment can safely be provided in a primary dental office.

Any suggested change to anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy before dental surgery should be made in consultation with and on the advice of the patient's physician.

For most patients receiving direct-acting oral anticoagulants, including dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, or edoxaban, limited evidence and general consensus indicate that no change in anticoagulant therapy is required for most dental interventions when usual local measures are used to control bleeding.

For patients considered to have a higher risk of bleeding because of comorbid conditions or more extensive procedures, possible approaches may include:

- postponing the daily anticoagulant dose until after the procedure;
- scheduling the dental intervention as late as possible after the most recent anticoagulant dose; or
- temporarily interrupting anticoagulant therapy for approximately 24 to 48 hours.

Such changes should be considered only in consultation with and on the advice of the patient's physician.

Further research is needed to establish definitive peri-procedural management strategies, particularly for patients considered to have a higher risk of bleeding.



# 3. Clinical Indications for Anticoagulant and Antiplatelet Therapy

## 3.1 Atrial Fibrillation [17]

Source: American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/American College of Clinical Pharmacy/Heart Rhythm Society (ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS).
2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS Guideline for the Diagnosis and Management of Atrial Fibrillation.
Published in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology in 2024.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is associated with an increased risk of stroke. Prevention of stroke is an important component of AF management.

### 3.1.1 Assessment of Thromboembolic Risk

Patients with AF should be evaluated for their annual risk of thromboembolic events using a validated clinical risk score, such as CHA2DS2-VASc.

**Class 1 recommendation; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

The CHA2DS2-VASc score includes the following clinical factors:

- congestive heart failure;
- hypertension;
- age 75 years or older, assigned 2 points;
- diabetes mellitus;
- prior stroke, transient ischemic attack, or thromboembolism, assigned 2 points;
- vascular disease;
- age 65 to 74 years; and
- female sex.

For patients with AF who have an estimated annual thromboembolic risk of 2% or greater per year, such as a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 2 or greater in men or 3 or greater in women, anticoagulation is recommended to prevent stroke and systemic thromboembolism.

**Class 1 recommendation; Level of Evidence A.**

For patients with AF who have an estimated annual thromboembolic risk of at least 1% but less than 2% per year, equivalent to a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 1 in men or 2 in women, anticoagulation is reasonable to prevent stroke and systemic thromboembolism.

**Class 2a recommendation; Level of Evidence A.**


### 3.1.2 Atrial Fibrillation Pattern and Stroke Prevention

For patients diagnosed with AF who have an estimated annual risk of stroke or thromboembolic events of 2% or greater, selection of therapy to reduce stroke risk should be based on the risk of thromboembolism regardless of whether the AF pattern is:

- paroxysmal;
- persistent;
- long-standing persistent; or
- permanent.

**Class 1 recommendation; Level of Evidence B-R.**

The need for and choice of stroke risk reduction therapy should be reevaluated periodically to reassess:

- stroke risk;
- bleeding risk;
- net clinical benefit; and
- appropriate dosing.

**Class 1 recommendation; Level of Evidence B-NR.**


### 3.1.3 Selection of Oral Anticoagulant Therapy

For patients with AF who do not have a history of moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis or a mechanical heart valve and who are candidates for anticoagulation, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are recommended over warfarin to reduce the risk of:

- mortality;
- stroke;
- systemic embolism; and
- intracranial hemorrhage.

**Class 1 recommendation; Level of Evidence A.**

Warfarin remains the first-line anticoagulant therapy for patients with AF and:

- moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis; or
- mechanical heart valves.


### 3.1.4 Atrial Fibrillation with Mitral Stenosis

For patients with rheumatic mitral stenosis, or mitral stenosis of moderate or greater severity, and a history of AF, long-term anticoagulation with warfarin is recommended over DOACs, independent of the CHA2DS2-VASc score, to prevent cardiovascular events including stroke or death.

**Class 1 recommendation; Level of Evidence B-R.**


### 3.1.5 Antiplatelet Therapy and Stroke Prevention in Atrial Fibrillation

For patients with AF who are candidates for anticoagulation and who do not have another indication for antiplatelet therapy, aspirin alone or aspirin in combination with clopidogrel is not recommended as an alternative to anticoagulation for reducing stroke risk.

**Class 3: Harm; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with AF who do not have risk factors for stroke, aspirin monotherapy for prevention of thromboembolic events provides no benefit.

**Class 3: No Benefit; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

Patients with AF may have a separate indication for antiplatelet therapy, such as coronary artery disease or vascular disease. In the absence of another indication for antiplatelet therapy, antiplatelet therapy should not be used for stroke-risk reduction in place of anticoagulation.


### 3.1.6 Factors Relevant to Bleeding Risk

Patients with AF should be evaluated for factors that specifically indicate a higher risk of bleeding, including:

- previous bleeding; and
- use of medications that increase bleeding risk.

The purpose of identifying these factors is to identify possible interventions that may prevent bleeding during anticoagulation.

**Class 1 recommendation; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

Bleeding risk scores should not be used in isolation to determine eligibility for oral anticoagulation in patients considered to be at high risk for stroke. Instead, bleeding risk assessment should be used to identify and modify bleeding risk factors and to inform medical decision-making.

**Class 3: No Benefit; Level of Evidence B-NR.**


### 3.1.7 Patient Factors Relevant to Oral Anticoagulant Management

Multiple factors should be considered when selecting an oral anticoagulant for a patient with AF. Major factors identified by the guideline include:

- efficacy;
- safety;
- insurance coverage;
- renal and hepatic function;
- drug interaction screening;
- medication adherence; and
- patient preferences.

DOAC dosing in patients with AF should be selected based on:

- age;
- renal function;
- body weight; and
- concomitant medications.

Hepatic function should also be evaluated when determining the appropriateness of DOAC therapy.

## 3.2 Venous Thromboembolism: Deep Vein Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism [18]

Source: Stevens et al. (2024), CHEST (American College of Chest Physicians).
Antithrombotic Therapy for VTE Disease: Compendium and Review of CHEST Guidelines 2012–2021.
Published in CHEST in 2024.

Source note: This 2024 compendium collects guidance statements considered relevant to current clinical care from the 2012 CHEST antithrombotic guideline and its 2016 and 2021 updates. The compendium itself is not an officially endorsed CHEST guideline; it serves as a unified collection of the contemporary guidance statements from the preceding CHEST guidelines.


### 3.2.1 Phases of Anticoagulant Therapy

CHEST organizes anticoagulant therapy for venous thromboembolism (VTE) into three phases:

- **Initiation phase (0–21 days):** the initial provision of anticoagulants following VTE diagnosis.
- **Treatment phase (3 months):** the period after initiation that completes treatment for the acute VTE event.
- **Extended phase (3 months to no planned stop date):** anticoagulant therapy given at full or reduced dose for secondary prevention.


### 3.2.2 Classification of Precipitating Factors for VTE

CHEST classifies VTE according to the presence or absence of precipitating factors as:

- VTE provoked by a **major transient risk factor**, present within 3 months before VTE diagnosis;
- VTE provoked by a **minor transient risk factor**, present within 2 months before VTE diagnosis;
- VTE provoked by a **persistent risk factor**; or
- **unprovoked VTE**.


### 3.2.3 Treatment-Phase Anticoagulation for Acute VTE

For patients with acute VTE who do not have a contraindication to anticoagulation, CHEST recommends a minimum 3-month treatment phase of anticoagulation.

**CHEST Statement 10.0 (2021): Strong Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**

At the conclusion of the treatment phase, patients should be assessed for whether extended-phase anticoagulation is needed.

For patients with acute VTE, defined in this recommendation as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) of the leg or pulmonary embolism (PE), CHEST recommends:

- apixaban;
- dabigatran;
- edoxaban; or
- rivaroxaban

over a vitamin K antagonist (VKA) for treatment-phase anticoagulation during the first 3 months.

**CHEST Statement 11.0 (2021): Strong Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**

The DOACs are listed alphabetically in the CHEST compendium. Their order does not indicate a preference for one DOAC over another.


### 3.2.4 Initiation of DOAC Therapy

When a DOAC is selected for treatment of VTE, CHEST notes that initiation differs according to the agent:

- apixaban and rivaroxaban are initiated using an initial higher-dose regimen; and
- dabigatran and edoxaban are initiated after an initial course of parenteral low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH).

The initiation phase is followed by the treatment-phase dose of the selected DOAC.


### 3.2.5 Extended-Phase Anticoagulation

The recommendation for extended-phase anticoagulation depends on the clinical context in which the VTE occurred.

For patients with VTE diagnosed in the setting of a **major transient risk factor**, CHEST recommends against offering extended-phase anticoagulation.

**CHEST Statement 12.0 (2021): Strong Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**

For patients with VTE diagnosed in the setting of a **minor transient risk factor**, CHEST suggests against offering extended-phase anticoagulation.

**CHEST Statement 12.1 (2021): Weak Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**

For patients with VTE diagnosed in the **absence of transient provocation (unprovoked VTE)** or VTE provoked by a **persistent risk factor**, CHEST recommends offering extended-phase anticoagulation with a DOAC.

**CHEST Statement 12.2 (2021): Strong Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**


### 3.2.6 Choice of Anticoagulant During Extended-Phase Therapy

For patients who are offered extended-phase anticoagulation, CHEST suggests reduced-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban over full-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban.

**CHEST Statement 13.0 (2021): Weak Recommendation, Very Low-Certainty Evidence.**

For patients with unprovoked VTE or VTE provoked by a persistent risk factor who cannot receive a DOAC, CHEST suggests extended-phase anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist.

**CHEST Statement 13.2 (2021): Weak Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**


### 3.2.7 VTE Associated With Cancer

For patients with acute VTE in the setting of cancer (cancer-associated thrombosis [CAT]), CHEST recommends an oral factor Xa inhibitor:

- apixaban;
- edoxaban; or
- rivaroxaban

over LMWH for the initiation and treatment phases of anticoagulant therapy.

**CHEST Statement 11.1 (2021): Strong Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**

Choice of anticoagulant in cancer-associated thrombosis is sensitive to patient factors including:

- drug-drug interactions;
- tolerance of oral medication; and
- cancer-specific treatment variables.

For patients with VTE and active cancer who do not have a high bleeding risk, CHEST recommends extended anticoagulant therapy with no scheduled stop date rather than stopping after 3 months.

**CHEST Statement 12.3 (2016): Strong Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**

For patients with VTE and active cancer who have a high bleeding risk, CHEST suggests extended anticoagulant therapy with no scheduled stop date rather than stopping after 3 months.

**CHEST Statement 12.3 (2016): Weak Recommendation, Moderate-Certainty Evidence.**


### 3.2.8 VTE and Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome

For patients with confirmed antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) who are being managed with anticoagulant therapy, CHEST suggests adjusted-dose vitamin K antagonist therapy, with a target INR of 2.5, over DOAC therapy during the treatment phase.

**CHEST Statement 20.0 (2021): Weak Recommendation, Low-Certainty Evidence.**

The CHEST panel notes that evidence showing lower efficacy of DOACs involved patients with triple-positive serologic profiles and that management of patients with other APS antibody profiles is less clear.



## 3.3 Acute Coronary Syndromes, PCI, and Coronary Stents in the ACS Setting [19]

Source: American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/American College of Emergency Physicians/National Association of EMS Physicians/Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI).
2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI Guideline for the Management of Patients With Acute Coronary Syndromes.
Published in Circulation in 2025.


### 3.3.1 Acute Coronary Syndrome

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is typically caused by disruption, through rupture or erosion, of an unstable coronary artery atherosclerotic plaque with associated partial or complete coronary artery thrombosis and/or microemboli. This can result in reduced blood flow to the myocardium and myocardial ischemia.

ACS includes three related clinical conditions:

- unstable angina;
- non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI); and
- ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).

The 2025 ACS guideline primarily focuses on ACS resulting from atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion and subsequent thrombosis.


### 3.3.2 Antiplatelet Therapy in Acute Coronary Syndrome

For patients with ACS, an initial oral loading dose of aspirin followed by daily low-dose aspirin is recommended to reduce death and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**

For patients with ACS, an oral P2Y12 inhibitor should be administered in addition to aspirin to reduce MACE.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**

Oral P2Y12 inhibitors addressed in the guideline include:

- clopidogrel;
- prasugrel; and
- ticagrelor.

The guideline notes that adding a P2Y12 inhibitor to aspirin reduces recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events but increases bleeding risk.


### 3.3.3 P2Y12 Inhibitor Selection in ACS and PCI

For patients with NSTE-ACS undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), prasugrel or ticagrelor is recommended to reduce MACE and stent thrombosis.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with NSTE-ACS, clopidogrel is recommended when prasugrel or ticagrelor:

- are unavailable;
- cannot be tolerated; or
- are contraindicated.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with STEMI managed with primary PCI, prasugrel or ticagrelor should be administered to reduce MACE and stent thrombosis.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with STEMI managed with primary PCI, clopidogrel is recommended when prasugrel or ticagrelor:

- are unavailable;
- cannot be tolerated; or
- are contraindicated.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence C-LD.**


### 3.3.4 Duration of Dual Antiplatelet Therapy After ACS

For patients with ACS who are not at high bleeding risk, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and an oral P2Y12 inhibitor should be administered for at least 1 year to reduce MACE.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**

The guideline identifies a minimum duration of 1 year of DAPT as the default strategy for patients with ACS who are not at high bleeding risk.


### 3.3.5 Patients With ACS Who Also Require Oral Anticoagulation

Patients with ACS may also have an indication for oral anticoagulant therapy, including:

- atrial fibrillation;
- venous thromboembolism; or
- prosthetic heart valves.

Triple antithrombotic therapy consists of:

- aspirin;
- a P2Y12 inhibitor; and
- an oral anticoagulant.

The guideline notes that bleeding risk is increased with triple antithrombotic therapy.

For patients with ACS who require oral anticoagulant therapy, aspirin should be discontinued after 1 to 4 weeks of triple antithrombotic therapy, with continued use of a P2Y12 inhibitor, preferably clopidogrel, and an oral anticoagulant to reduce bleeding risk.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-R.**


## 3.4 Valvular Heart Disease and Prosthetic Heart Valves [20]

Source: American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA).
2020 ACC/AHA Guideline for the Management of Patients With Valvular Heart Disease.
Published in Circulation in 2021.


### 3.4.1 Mechanical Prosthetic Valves and Anticoagulation

For patients with a mechanical prosthetic valve, anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist (VKA) is recommended.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**

The guideline states that all patients with mechanical valves require lifelong anticoagulant therapy with a VKA to reduce the risk of valve thrombosis and thromboembolic events.

The INR goal depends on the type and position of the mechanical valve and the presence of additional thromboembolic risk factors.


### 3.4.2 INR Targets for Mechanical Prosthetic Valves

For patients with a mechanical bileaflet or current-generation single-tilting disk aortic valve replacement (AVR) and no risk factors for thromboembolism, VKA anticoagulation to achieve an INR of 2.5 is recommended.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

For patients with a mechanical AVR and additional thromboembolic risk factors, or with an older-generation prosthesis, VKA anticoagulation to achieve an INR of 3.0 is indicated.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

Thromboembolic risk factors identified by the guideline include:

- atrial fibrillation;
- previous thromboembolism;
- left ventricular dysfunction; and
- a hypercoagulable state.

An older-generation prosthesis, such as a ball-in-cage valve, is also associated with the recommendation for the higher INR target.

For patients with a mechanical mitral valve replacement, VKA anticoagulation to achieve an INR of 3.0 is indicated.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-NR.**


### 3.4.3 On-X Mechanical Aortic Valve

For patients with a mechanical On-X aortic valve replacement and no thromboembolic risk factors, use of a VKA targeted to a lower INR of 1.5 to 2.0 may be reasonable beginning at least 3 months after surgery, with continuation of aspirin 75 to 100 mg daily.

**Class of Recommendation 2b; Level of Evidence B-R.**


### 3.4.4 Direct Oral Anticoagulants and Mechanical Prosthetic Valves

For patients with a mechanical valve prosthesis, anticoagulation with the direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran is contraindicated.

**Class of Recommendation 3: Harm; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with a mechanical valve prosthesis, the use of anti-Xa direct oral anticoagulants has not been assessed and is not recommended.

**Class of Recommendation 3: Harm; Level of Evidence C-EO.**

Vitamin K antagonists remain the anticoagulant therapy used for patients with mechanical heart valves.


### 3.4.5 Antithrombotic Therapy After Bioprosthetic Valve Replacement

For patients with a bioprosthetic transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), aspirin 75 to 100 mg daily is reasonable in the absence of another indication for oral anticoagulation.

**Class of Recommendation 2a; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with a bioprosthetic surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) or bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement, aspirin 75 to 100 mg daily is reasonable in the absence of another indication for oral anticoagulation.

**Class of Recommendation 2a; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

For patients with a bioprosthetic SAVR or mitral valve replacement who are at low risk of bleeding, VKA anticoagulation to achieve an INR of 2.5 is reasonable for at least 3 months and for as long as 6 months after surgical valve replacement.

**Class of Recommendation 2a; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

The guideline notes that many patients with a surgically implanted bioprosthetic aortic or mitral valve will not require lifelong anticoagulation in the absence of an independent indication for anticoagulation, such as atrial fibrillation.


### 3.4.6 Atrial Fibrillation in Patients With Valvular Heart Disease

For patients with atrial fibrillation and native valvular heart disease, except rheumatic mitral stenosis, or patients who received a bioprosthetic valve more than 3 months previously, a non-vitamin K oral anticoagulant (NOAC) is an effective alternative to VKA anticoagulation and should be administered on the basis of the patient's CHA2DS2-VASc score.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**

For patients with atrial fibrillation and rheumatic mitral stenosis, long-term VKA oral anticoagulation is recommended.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence C-EO.**

For patients with new-onset atrial fibrillation within 3 months after surgical or transcatheter bioprosthetic valve replacement, anticoagulation with a VKA is reasonable.

**Class of Recommendation 2a; Level of Evidence B-NR.**

For patients with mechanical heart valves, with or without atrial fibrillation, who require long-term VKA anticoagulation to prevent valve thrombosis, NOACs are not recommended.

**Class of Recommendation 3: Harm; Level of Evidence B-R.**


### 3.4.7 Mechanical Prosthetic Valves and Minor Dental Procedures

For patients with mechanical heart valves who are undergoing minor procedures, including dental extractions where bleeding can be readily controlled, continuation of VKA anticoagulation with a therapeutic INR is recommended.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence C-EO.**

The guideline also states that antithrombotic therapy should not be stopped for procedures in which bleeding is unlikely or would be inconsequential, including:

- dental cleaning; and
- simple treatment for dental caries.



## 3.5 Ischemic Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack [21]

Source: American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA).
2021 Guideline for the Prevention of Stroke in Patients With Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack.
Published in Stroke in 2021.

Source scope: This guideline addresses secondary prevention of ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA). It does not address acute management of ischemic stroke.

The guideline notes that secondary prevention strategies often depend on the subtype and etiology of the ischemic stroke or TIA.

In general, with very few exceptions, patients with TIA and patients with ischemic stroke are treated similarly for purposes of secondary prevention.


### 3.5.1 Antithrombotic Therapy After Noncardioembolic Ischemic Stroke or TIA

For patients with noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or TIA, antiplatelet therapy is indicated in preference to oral anticoagulation to reduce the risk of recurrent ischemic stroke and other cardiovascular events while minimizing the risk of bleeding.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**

For patients with noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or TIA, the following antiplatelet regimens are indicated for secondary prevention of ischemic stroke:

- aspirin 50 to 325 mg daily;
- clopidogrel 75 mg daily; or
- aspirin 25 mg combined with extended-release dipyridamole 200 mg twice daily.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**


### 3.5.2 Short-Term Dual Antiplatelet Therapy After Minor Ischemic Stroke or High-Risk TIA

For patients with a recent minor noncardioembolic ischemic stroke, defined as a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 3 or less, or a high-risk TIA, defined as an ABCD2 score of 4 or greater, dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel should be initiated early.

The guideline specifies initiation:

- ideally within 12 to 24 hours of symptom onset; and
- at least within 7 days of symptom onset.

Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel should be continued for 21 to 90 days and then followed by single antiplatelet therapy to reduce the risk of recurrent ischemic stroke.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A (Systematic Review).**


### 3.5.3 Ticagrelor Plus Aspirin in Selected Patients

For patients with a recent event within 24 hours who have:

- minor to moderate stroke with an NIHSS score of 5 or less;
- high-risk TIA with an ABCD2 score of 6 or greater; or
- symptomatic intracranial or extracranial stenosis of 30% or greater in an artery that could account for the event,

dual antiplatelet therapy with ticagrelor plus aspirin for 30 days may be considered to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke within 30 days.

The guideline states that this regimen may also increase the risk of serious bleeding events, including intracranial hemorrhage.

**Class of Recommendation 2b; Level of Evidence B-R (Systematic Review).**


### 3.5.4 Long-Term Dual or Triple Antiplatelet Therapy

For patients with noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or TIA, continuous use of dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel for more than 90 days, or the use of triple antiplatelet therapy, is associated with an excess risk of hemorrhage.

**Class of Recommendation 3: Harm; Level of Evidence A (Systematic Review).**

The guideline notes that there may be non-stroke indications for dual antiplatelet therapy beyond 90 days, such as recent drug-eluting coronary stent placement. These indications should be distinguished from dual antiplatelet therapy being used specifically for secondary stroke prevention.


### 3.5.5 Ischemic Stroke or TIA Associated With Atrial Fibrillation

For patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation and stroke or TIA, oral anticoagulation is recommended to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke.

Oral anticoagulant options identified in the guideline include:

- apixaban;
- dabigatran;
- edoxaban;
- rivaroxaban; and
- warfarin.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence A.**

For patients with stroke or TIA and atrial fibrillation who do not have moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis or a mechanical heart valve, apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, or rivaroxaban is recommended in preference to warfarin to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with atrial fibrillation and stroke or TIA, oral anticoagulation is indicated to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke regardless of whether the atrial fibrillation pattern is:

- paroxysmal;
- persistent; or
- permanent.

**Class of Recommendation 1; Level of Evidence B-R.**


### 3.5.6 Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source

The guideline defines embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS) as a nonlacunar stroke without an obvious source after a minimum standard evaluation to exclude known stroke etiologies.

For patients with ESUS, treatment with direct oral anticoagulants is not recommended to reduce the risk of secondary stroke.

**Class of Recommendation 3: No Benefit; Level of Evidence B-R.**

For patients with ESUS, treatment with ticagrelor is not recommended to reduce the risk of secondary stroke.

**Class of Recommendation 3: No Benefit; Level of Evidence B-NR.**
